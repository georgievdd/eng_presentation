# Speaker Notes: Body Language in Japan

---

## Slide 1 — Title

Hello everyone. Today I want to talk about body language in Japan.
Japan is a very interesting country when it comes to communication.
People there use gestures, silence, and posture to express things that they never say with words.
Let's take a look at what makes Japanese body language so unique.

---

## Slide 2 — Introduction

Japan is what we call a "high-context culture."
This means that most communication happens without words.
The way you stand, how you look at someone, and even how long you stay silent — all of this sends a message.

In Western countries, we usually say things directly.
In Japan, it is the opposite. You need to read between the lines.
If you don't understand Japanese body language, you can easily offend someone without knowing it.

---

## Slide 3 — Bowing

The most basic greeting in Japan is the bow. There is no handshake between Japanese people.
But bowing is not simple — the angle matters a lot.

A small bow of about 15 degrees is just a casual hello, like when you pass someone in the hallway.
A 30-degree bow is more formal. You do this when meeting someone for the first time or showing respect.
A 45-degree bow is for serious situations — a deep apology or greeting someone very important.

Also, the person with lower status always bows first and bows deeper.
So if you are a student and you meet your professor, you bow first and lower.
This shows who has more respect for whom.

---

## Slide 4 — Eye Contact

In Western countries, eye contact shows that you are confident and honest.
But in Japan, it is completely different.

Direct eye contact is seen as aggressive or even rude.
When Japanese people talk to each other, they usually look at the other person's neck or chest area — not directly into the eyes.
Children in Japan are taught from a young age: do not stare at people.

So if you visit Japan and try to show confidence by looking someone in the eyes, they might actually feel uncomfortable.
It is a small thing, but it changes everything about how people feel in a conversation.

---

## Slide 5 — Nodding and Silence

Here is something that surprises many foreigners.
In Japan, when someone nods and says "hai" — which means "yes" — it does NOT mean they agree with you.
It only means "I am listening. I hear you."

This is called aizuchi. It is a way of showing that you are paying attention.
Many foreign businesspeople make the mistake of thinking the deal is done when they hear a lot of "hai." But it is not.

Also, silence in Japan is not awkward. It is respectful.
If someone goes quiet for a moment before answering, they are thinking carefully.
They are being respectful, not rude.
In Western culture, we sometimes feel nervous when there is silence and try to fill it.
In Japan, silence is perfectly normal and even polite.

---

## Slide 6 — Hand Gestures

Let me show you some hand gestures that are different in Japan.

First — "come here." In Western countries, we do this with the palm facing up and fingers pointing toward us.
In Japan, it is the opposite — the palm faces DOWN and the fingers wave downward.
If you use the Western gesture in Japan, it looks strange or even offensive.

Second — "that's me." When a Japanese person wants to say "I" or point to themselves, they point to their NOSE.
Not to their chest like we do. Just to the nose.

Third — pointing at someone. Pointing with one finger is considered rude in Japan.
Instead, people use an open hand — all fingers together — to indicate a direction or a person.

And finally — "no" or "forbidden." Japanese people wave their hand in front of their face, or cross their arms in an X shape to say no.

---

## Slide 7 — Personal Space and Touch

Personal space in Japan is much bigger than in many other countries.
Strangers do not touch each other. There is no hugging, no kiss on the cheek, no pat on the shoulder.

If you are from Southern Europe or Latin America, where people stand close and touch often during conversation, Japan will feel very different.

Japanese people will shake hands with foreigners because they know it is a Western custom.
But they rarely start a handshake themselves.

The rule is simple: keep your distance and do not touch unless the other person clearly expects it.

---

## Slide 8 — Business Card Ritual

Now, one of my favorite examples — the business card.
In Japan, exchanging business cards is a real ritual, not just a quick swap.

When you give your card, you use BOTH hands and present it with the text facing the other person so they can read it immediately.
When you receive a card, you also take it with both hands.
Then you read it carefully. You don't just put it in your pocket.

During the meeting, you place the card on the table in front of you.
You never write on it, you never fold it, and you absolutely never put it in your back pocket.

Why? Because in Japan, the business card represents the person.
If you treat the card badly, you are treating the person badly.
This might seem small to us, but in Japan it is a very big deal.

---

## Slide 9 — Common Misunderstandings

Let's talk about three things that foreigners often misunderstand.

First — smiling. We usually think a smile means someone is happy.
But in Japan, a smile can also mean the person is embarrassed, nervous, or uncomfortable.
So if a Japanese person smiles and says nothing, it might not mean everything is fine.

Second — "hai." As I said earlier, it means "I'm listening," not "I agree."
Many businesspeople from other countries leave a meeting thinking the Japanese side said yes to everything.
But actually, nothing was confirmed.

Third — laughing. If a Japanese person laughs in an uncomfortable or serious situation, it is usually a nervous reaction.
It does not mean they think something is funny.
Don't take it personally — they are just trying to deal with the awkward moment.

---

## Slide 10 — Comparison Table

Let me quickly compare Japan and Western countries side by side.

For greetings — Japan uses bowing, the West uses handshakes or hugs.
Eye contact — in Japan you avoid it, in the West you maintain it.
Touching — in Japan it is rare, in many Western countries it is common.
Silence — in Japan it is comfortable and respectful, in the West it often feels awkward.
Nodding — in Japan it means "I'm listening," in the West it means "I agree."
Pointing — in Japan you use an open hand, in the West one finger is fine.
Smiling — in Japan it can mean discomfort, in the West it usually means happiness.

As you can see, almost everything is different. That is why it is so easy to make mistakes.

---

## Slide 11 — Why It Matters

So why should we care about all this?

First — business. If you work with Japanese companies or clients, knowing these things can save a deal.
A small mistake like too much eye contact or the wrong gesture can make the other side feel disrespected.

Second — travel. If you visit Japan and you know the rules, your experience will be much better.
You will not accidentally offend anyone.

Third — respect. Learning about another culture's body language shows that you care.
It shows that you made an effort.

Here is a real example: A foreign businessman kept looking his Japanese partners directly in the eyes to show confidence.
The Japanese partners felt uncomfortable and thought he was being aggressive.
The meeting almost failed — not because of the product, but because of eye contact.
One small thing, big consequences.

---

## Slide 12 — Conclusion

So, to finish — what are the three main things to remember?

One: in Japan, restraint means respect. Doing less is often better than doing more.
Two: silence and small gestures carry a lot of meaning. Pay attention to them.
Three: learning body language means learning the culture. You cannot separate them.

Japan is a fascinating country. And once you understand how people communicate there,
you start to see that they actually express a lot — just in a very quiet way.

Thank you for listening. Does anyone have questions?
